# Portfolio allocation

How to structure the long-term investment portfolio once high-interest debt is
cleared and an emergency fund is in place (see `financial-priorities`).

- **Invest early and consistently.** Time in the market and compounding matter
  more than timing. Start as soon as the foundations are set and contribute
  regularly.
- **Follow the index; don't pick individual stocks.** Over the long run it is
  very hard to beat the market, so prefer low-expense-ratio index ETFs or mutual
  funds — e.g. a total-market fund like the Vanguard Total Stock Market fund —
  over stock-picking or high-fee active funds. Diversify across regions and
  company sizes (caps).
- **Small tilts only, if any.** A deliberate overweight to a sector or factor —
  small cap, tech, real estate — is acceptable *only* with high conviction, and
  kept small: on the order of **5–10% of the total portfolio**, not a core
  holding.
- **Avoid concentrated single-stock risk, especially employer stock.** Holding a
  lot of your employer's stock for the long term doubles your exposure — your job
  *and* your investments ride on one company. Diversify out of concentrated
  positions rather than letting them dominate the portfolio.
- **Stock/bond split — rule of thumb:** target **(110 − age)% in stocks**, the
  rest in bonds. Example: at age 30, ≈ 80% stocks / 20% bonds. Shift more toward
  bonds as you age or if your risk tolerance is lower; toward stocks if higher
  and the horizon is long.
- **Rebalance** back toward the target when a class drifts meaningfully from it,
  so the portfolio doesn't quietly become riskier or more conservative than
  intended.
- **Ground it in real holdings:** use the user's actual investment accounts and
  current allocation (available via the investment tools) when comparing to
  these targets or suggesting changes.

General education, not personalized advice.
